\begin{abstract}
% TODO WS5 (write last, ~150-200 words): objectives, approach, key findings.
Independent corner stores struggle to digitise inventory for online sale because creating a
catalogue entry per product is slow and manual. We present Snap-to-Sell, an open-source pipeline
that turns a single phone photo of a packaged product into a publishable listing: it recognises
the item with a vision--language model fused with packaging OCR, retrieves a canonical name,
price, and product image from open product catalogues and a shopping-search backend, generates
grounded listing copy, replaces the shelf photo with an LLM-verified same-product catalogue image,
and applies responsible-AI guardrails (age-restricted-item flagging, hallucination and
low-confidence routing) before human review. We evaluate the pipeline on a hand-labelled set of
58 corner-store products spanning food, beverage, household, personal-care, tobacco, and alcohol
categories, measuring identification accuracy, price error, age-restriction recall, and the
image-upgrade rate against a naive vision--language caption baseline. Snap-to-Sell improves top-1
identification (86.2\% vs.\ 84.5\%) and category accuracy (79.3\% vs.\ 77.6\%), flags 100\% of
age-restricted items, and upgrades 29\% of images, while its retrieval-grounded prices are less
accurate than the baseline's direct estimate on low-value fast-moving goods (43.1\% vs.\ 22.4\%
median absolute error)---a failure mode we trace to multipack and look-alike matches and mitigate
by declining to price and routing low-confidence matches to human review.
\end{abstract}

\section{Related Work}
% Prose drafted in ../related_work_draft.md — condense to fit. Citations resolve from
% references.bib (verify the entries tagged TODO before submission).
Automatically turning a product photograph into a priced listing draws on four established
threads: listing-text generation, fine-grained product recognition, price estimation, and
product image retrieval. Recent listing-generation work favours frozen foundation models: ModICT
generates descriptions from an image and keywords using a frozen language model with in-context
references, and names the failure we guard against---generic, inaccurate copy for same-category
products~\cite{li2024modict,kathiriya2023listing}. Fine-grained recognition of packaged goods is well
supported by large datasets such as RP2K and the smartphone-captured CAPG-GP
collection~\cite{peng2020rp2k,geng2018oneshot}, and fusing the product image with packaging OCR
outperforms image-only recognition, especially with few samples~\cite{grocery2023ocr}. Price
estimation has moved from image-only regression~\cite{chen2018priceright,secondhand2020deep}
toward retrieval-grounded, confidence-aware generation~\cite{llp2025}. Finally, the image-upgrade
step rests on product visual search and same-product verification~\cite{sir2020,shin2022eclip},
which also supply restricted-item detection reusable for our age-restriction guardrail.

\section{Results}
We score the full pipeline against a naive baseline---the same recognition stage followed by
ungrounded listing generation (no retrieval, image upgrade, or guardrails)---on 58 hand-labelled
products, each with a real observed price. Both systems are evaluated with identical metrics;
price error is the absolute percentage difference between the predicted and the observed price.
Table~\ref{tab:results} reports the headline figures.

\begin{table}[t]
\caption{Performance on the 58-item corner-store test set. Lower is better for price error; the
better system per row is shown in bold.}
\label{tab:results}
\centering
\begin{tabular}{@{}lcc@{}}
\toprule
Metric & Baseline & Snap-to-Sell \\
\midrule
Top-1 identification accuracy & 84.5\% & \textbf{86.2\%} \\
Category accuracy & 77.6\% & \textbf{79.3\%} \\
Age-restriction recall & 0\% & \textbf{100\%} \\
Image-upgrade (swap) rate & 0\% & \textbf{29.3\%} \\
Price median abs.\ \% error & \textbf{22.4\%} & 43.1\% \\
Price mean abs.\ \% error & \textbf{47.4\%} & 359.9\% \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Snap-to-Sell outperforms the baseline on four of the five measured axes: it recognises the exact
product slightly more often, assigns the correct category more often, flags every age-restricted
item (tobacco and alcohol) where the baseline flags none, and upgrades roughly one image in three
to a verified same-product catalogue photo. The remaining axis, price, is the one clear failure
mode: the baseline's direct price estimate is more accurate than our retrieval-grounded price on
these low-value fast-moving goods. Inspection of the per-item logs attributes this to the shopping
backend returning multipack or case listings and visually similar look-alikes for cheap
single-unit items, so a plausible-looking but wrong comparable anchors the price. The confidence
gate mitigates the worst cases by declining to price and routing to review the eight items whose
retrieved brand could not be confirmed; the reported price figures are computed over the items
each system did price, so the two price columns cover slightly different subsets. Top-3 accuracy
and a blind human listing-quality rating were not automated for this run and are left to future
work.
